Chapter 1 · Types of money

Money is four IOUs, not one

Currency, reserves, deposits and Treasuries come from different issuers. All trade at par, until the conversion pipe breaks.

Real-life case

March 2020: the Treasury “ATM” broke

Funds and foreign central banks raced to raise dollars and sold Treasuries. Dealers hit their balance-sheet limits and stopped buying. Even the safest bond couldn’t be sold at a fair price, until the Fed became the buyer.

  • Mar 15, 2020: Fed announces it will buy at least $500B Treasuries and $200B agency MBS CHECKED≥ $700B
  • Bought by mid-June 2020: Treasuries / agency MBS (Fed Monetary Policy Report) CHECKED$1.6T / $719B
  • Peak daily pace, Treasuries CHECKED~$75B/day
  • Why dealers stalled: balance-sheet limits (book blames post-2008 rules) BOOK—
Lesson: a Treasury only counts as “money” if you can turn it into a bank deposit at once. In March 2020 you couldn’t.
NY Fed research agrees that dealer balance sheets were the bottleneck. Whether post-2008 regulation was a main cause is debated; I did not check that point.

Key actors · who holds what, and why
Remember these 3
Four monies, one rule. They must convert into each other at par. Crises are conversion failures.
Only the Fed creates reserves; banks create deposits when they lend. Reserves never leave the Fed. They only move between accounts.
Treasuries are money for big investors, but their price floats, so “moneyness” is not perfect.
Common traps
✗ “Banks lend out the deposits they collect.”✓ The book’s view: a loan creates a new deposit; deposits are destroyed when loans are repaid.
✗ “QE gave banks money to lend.”✓ Reserves can’t leave the Fed. Lending is limited by regulation and by finding profitable borrowers.
✗ “Cash on the sidelines will flood the stock market.”✓ Buying a stock moves a deposit from you to the seller. Total deposits don’t change.
✗ “A central bank digital currency would clearly be safer and faster.”✓ OPINION The author argues the benefits are illusory and the privacy cost is high. Supporters disagree; I did not review that literature.

So what? (investor lens) MY ADD

Treasuries are the cash-like asset big investors park money in, and the base for the risk-free rate in valuation. Above $250k, a deposit is an unsecured credit exposure to the bank (see tab 2).

Book vs today

  • Reserves: book shows $1.68T held by banks (Jan 2020). Fed H.4.1 reserve balances were about $3.0T (week avg ending Sept 16, 2026) CHECKED
Chapter 2 · The money creators

Fed, banks, Treasury: three creators, three constraints

The Fed creates reserves, banks create deposits, the Treasury creates securities. A bank’s real problem is staying solvent and liquid.

Real-life case MY ADD

Silicon Valley Bank, March 2023: a quarter of deposits gone in a day

A bank funded mostly by uninsured, institutional deposits held securities that had lost value. Once clients doubted it, they ran.

  • Mar 9, 2023: withdrawal requests, about a quarter of deposits (Fed’s Barr; CNBC) CHECKED$42B
  • Mar 10: bank fails; further requests waiting that morning CHECKED~$100B more
  • Uninsured share of deposits: sources differ (Dec 2022 call report vs S&P/Yale) CHECKED86–94%
  • Drivers per a Fed-commissioned review, reported Sept 2026: unrealised losses above capital, run-prone deposits, not ready to borrow at the discount window CHECKED—
Lesson: solvency (are assets worth more than debts?) and liquidity (can you pay today?) are different tests. Banks can fail either one.
The last row comes from a press summary (ANI/Tribune) of the review, not from the report itself.

Remember these 3
Lending creates deposits. Reserves are only for settling between banks.
Two ways to die: insolvent (bad assets) or illiquid (can’t pay today). Solvency needs capital; liquidity needs a lender.
The limits are regulation and profit, so banks create more money in booms and less in recessions.
Common traps
✗ “A bank needs reserves before it can lend.”✓ Book: it lends first and borrows reserves later if needed.
✗ “The discount window is a normal funding line.”✓ It is the last resort because using it signals distress.
✗ “Rising US debt means a crisis is imminent.”✓ OPINION The book notes Japan runs far higher debt and yields fell. It also says a limit exists but is unclear. Contested.
✗ “Big QE always lifts inflation.”✓ BOOK The Fed, BoJ and ECB missed targets for a decade despite QE. That line is dated: US inflation later ran well above 2%. I did not pull the figures for this capsule.

So what? (deal lens) MY ADD

Banks run at roughly 20x leverage, so small asset losses hit equity fast. That is why bank deals are priced off tangible equity, and why uninsured institutional deposits leave first.

Chapter 3 · The shadow banks

Banking without the safety net

Shadow banks borrow short and hold longer assets like banks do, but they have no deposit insurance and no Fed discount window.

Real-life case

Reserve Primary Fund, September 2008: a 3-cent loss starts a run

A money fund that investors treated like a bank account held a little Lehman debt. When Lehman failed, the fund could not pay out a full $1 per share.

  • Fund size CHECKED$65B
  • Lehman debt held (about 1.2% of assets) CHECKED$785M
  • Sept 16, 2008: value per share falls from $1.00 to CHECKED$0.97
  • Withdrawn from prime money funds across the industry in the next 2 days CHECKED~$200B
  • Sept 19: Treasury guarantees money funds; Fed announces a liquidity facility CHECKED—
Lesson: if investors believe “$1 in = $1 out” and it fails once, everyone rushes the exit at the same time.
The book says $42B left a fund that had $65B, and names the Fed’s Money Market Investor Funding Facility. An early industry report shows ~$27B out in two days, and the facility announced on Sept 19 was a different one (AMLF, per IMMFA). I could not reconcile the outflow figure and did not verify the MMIFF date.

Key actors · the shadow banking zoo
Remember these 3
Shadow bank = short funding, longer assets, no safety net. That is the recipe for runs.
2008 and March 2020 were runs on the shadow system. The Fed reached it through the primary dealers.
Leverage magnifies small price moves. At 5x, a 10% fall removes 60% of equity.
Common traps
✗ “Shadow banks are illegal or hidden.”✓ They are non-banks doing bank-like lending, with lighter rules and no public backstop.
✗ “ETFs can’t suffer runs.”✓ Share prices can fall far below asset value when the underlying bonds don’t trade (March 2020, BOOK).
✗ “Money funds are as safe as bank deposits.”✓ No FDIC, no discount window. Government funds hold only government paper; prime funds lend to banks too.
✗ “The Fed only rescued banks.”✓ Through primary dealers and emergency facilities it also backstopped shadow banks (book’s argument, BOOK).

So what? (deal lens) MY ADD

Repo, securitization and money-fund funding are the plumbing behind ABS and credit markets. The common failure pattern is the same: leverage plus short-term funding plus a price shock.

Chapter 4 · The Eurodollar market

Dollars outside the US, still tied to the US

Eurodollars are dollars held or created outside the US. The Fed doesn’t supervise that system, but it ends up backing it.

Real-life case

BNP Paribas, 2014: a French bank punished in New York

BNP moved dollars for clients in Sudan, Iran and Cuba. Because every dollar payment touches the US banking system, US authorities could hit a non-US bank.

  • June 30, 2014: guilty plea; total penalties CHECKED≈ $8.9B
  • Value of dollar-clearing for sanctioned parties (about a decade, to 2012) CHECKED> $190B
  • Settlement also included a temporary ban on some dollar clearing (NPR/WSJ) CHECKED—
Lesson: the dollar is a network. Whoever controls the US end can cut you off.
The book rounds the fine to $9B. It also says Iran must sell oil for gold; I could not verify that, so it is left out.

Key actors
Remember these 3
Foreign dollar demand creates offshore dollar banks. Reasons: safety, trade invoicing, cheap funding, deep markets.
Every dollar links back to the US banking system. That gives the US leverage (sanctions) and responsibility (swap lines).
Offshore funding is institutional and flighty. In stress the Fed lends through foreign central banks.
Common traps
✗ “Eurodollars are euros.”✓ They are US dollars held outside the US. The name dates from Europe in the 1950s.
✗ “Offshore dollars are just US money coming back.”✓ Book: that was true of the regulatory-arbitrage era. Now most flows are foreign to foreign.
✗ “The dollar’s role is permanent.”✓ OPINION Its reserve share fell from ~71% to ~57%. The book argues no rival has comparable market depth; others disagree.
✗ “The Fed lends only to US banks.”✓ Via swap lines it lent to foreign central banks in 2008 and 2020. Peak 2020 use: $449B (week of May 27) CHECKED. The book says “almost $500B”.

So what? (deal lens) MY ADD

Offshore dollar bonds are usually issued under English or New York law BOOK. That is the international debt capital markets channel, and its cost follows Fed rates and bank funding rules.
